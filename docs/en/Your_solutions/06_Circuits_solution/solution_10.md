# 10. Quantitative Analysis of Average Current in a Lightning Bolt

**Problem Statement:** A lightning bolt transfers a total electric charge of $Q = 30 \, C$ (Coulombs) from the atmosphere to the ground. This phenomenon occurs within an extremely brief time interval of $\Delta t = 2 \, ms$ (milliseconds). Our objective is to determine the magnitude of the **average current** ($I_{avg}$) generated during this discharge.

---

### **I. Theoretical Definition of Average Current**
While instantaneous current requires differentiation, **average current** represents the total amount of charge passing through a point divided by the total time duration of the flow. It provides a macroscopic view of the charge movement over the entire event.
* **Fundamental Formula:**
$$I_{avg} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}$$
Where:
* $I_{avg}$ is the average current in **Amperes (A)**.
* $\Delta Q$ is the total charge in **Coulombs (C)**.
* $\Delta t$ is the time interval in **Seconds (s)**.

---

### **II. Dimensional Analysis and Unit Conversion**
In physics, consistency in SI units is mandatory. The time given in the problem is in **milliseconds**, which must be converted to **seconds** before performing the calculation.
* **Conversion Factor:** $1 \, s = 1000 \, ms \implies 1 \, ms = 10^{-3} \, s$
* **Substitution:**
$$\Delta t = 2 \, ms = 2 \times 10^{-3} \, s = 0.002 \, s$$

---

### **III. Step-by-Step Numerical Computation**
By substituting the standardized values into our primary equation:
1. **Identify Variables:** $Q = 30 \, C$ and $t = 0.002 \, s$.
2. **Apply Formula:**
$$I_{avg} = \frac{30 \, C}{0.002 \, s}$$
3. **Calculation:**
Multiplying both the numerator and denominator by 1000 to simplify:
$$I_{avg} = \frac{30,000}{2} = 15,000 \, A$$

**Final Result:** The average current of the lightning bolt is **$15,000 \, A$** (or **$15 \, kA$**).

---

### **IV. Physical Significance**
The result of $15,000 \, A$ highlights the immense power involved in atmospheric discharges. To put this in perspective, a typical household circuit operates at $16 \, A$ to $32 \, A$. The lightning bolt in this problem carries nearly **500 to 1000 times** the current of a standard home system, but only for a fraction of a second.
